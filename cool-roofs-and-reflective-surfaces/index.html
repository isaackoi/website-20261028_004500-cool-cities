Cool Roofs and Reflective Surfaces: Turning Down the Urban Thermostat

A roof is a decision about sunlight. Every square metre of conventional dark roofing absorbs most of the solar energy that strikes it and converts that energy into heat — heat that flows into the rooms below, into the air above, and into the electricity bill of the building's owner.

Introduction

A cool roof makes the opposite decision. By reflecting more sunlight and shedding the heat it does absorb, it stays dramatically cooler, lowers indoor temperatures in buildings without air-conditioning, cuts peak cooling demand where air-conditioning exists, and — deployed across thousands of buildings — measurably cools the city around it. Cool roofing is one of the few urban climate strategies that is simultaneously a retrofit (a bucket of coating), a building code, and a global climate lever.[1][2]

This article, the second in the Cool Cities series on urban heat, explains how reflective surfaces work, what they achieve at building and city scale, where their limits are, and why codes and rebate programmes now treat roof colour as public infrastructure.

The physics: reflect it, or re-emit it

Two properties determine how hot a roof runs in the sun.[1]

Solar reflectance — albedo — is the fraction of incoming sunlight a surface bounces away. A fresh dark membrane reflects very little; it absorbs most sunlight and becomes a radiator. A bright white coating can reflect the large majority of solar energy, which never becomes heat at all. Reflectance is the dominant factor in how hot the surface gets.

Thermal emittance is how efficiently a surface re-radiates the heat it does absorb, mainly as infrared energy. A high-emittance surface dumps stored heat back toward the sky quickly, which matters most in warm, sunny climates and especially on calm clear nights.

The two together explain surface temperatures: a material that neither reflects nor emits well becomes the hottest object in the landscape. This is why dark, unshaded asphalt and bituminous roofing dominate thermal images of cities.[1][3]

What a cool roof is, in practice

Cool roofing is a product category, not a single material, and it exists for both flat commercial roofs and pitched residential ones.[1]

On low-slope roofs (flatter than about 2:12), the standard options include liquid-applied reflective coatings; single-ply membranes made with cool-coloured or white sheeting; light-coloured gravel aggregate ballast; and reflective granule-surfaced asphaltic membranes.

On steep-slope residential roofs, manufacturers now offer asphalt shingles made with infrared-reflective granules that look like ordinary colours but reflect the near-infrared portion of sunlight — the majority of solar energy — that conventional pigments absorb. Terra cotta tiles are naturally cool; glazed and polymer-coated tiles, reflective metal shingles, and composite shingles with cool pigments extend the palette. So-called “directionally reflective” products even change apparent colour with viewing angle to balance reflectance against appearance.

Maintenance matters: low-slope roofs need periodic cleaning because soiling erodes reflectance over time, and products with special reflective pigments carry a price premium.[1]

What the measurements show, building by building

The US Environmental Protection Agency’s synthesis of field studies quantifies the indoor effects.[1]

In residential buildings without air-conditioning, a cool roof lowers maximum indoor temperatures by 1.2–3.3°C (2.2–5.9°F) — often the difference between a bedroom that recovers at night and one that does not.

In air-conditioned residential buildings, the reflectance gain reduces peak cooling demand by 11–27%. Because utility grids strain at the peak — and because electricity for cooling is itself a source of urban waste heat — this demand reduction compounds: every cooled building dumps less heat into the street.

These numbers echo the longer research literature. Since foundational work in the late 1990s and early 2000s established that cool surfaces and shade trees measurably cut building cooling loads and urban air temperatures, field and modelling studies have repeatedly confirmed the pattern across climates.[3]

From one roof to a whole city

The more consequential effects appear when reflective roofing is combined with lighter pavement across a district or city. This is where individual retrofits become climate policy.[2][4]

A widely cited global analysis by Hashem Akbari, Surabi Menon and Arthur Rosenfeld calculated that increasing the albedo of urban roofs and pavements in tropical and temperate cities worldwide — a realistic retrofit of the existing stock — would produce a one-time offset of about 44 billion tonnes of CO2, because reflecting solar energy back to space counteracts a portion of greenhouse forcing. Follow-up modelling of the radiative forcing from urban albedo changes confirmed a small but genuinely global cooling effect, alongside the large local ones.[2][4]

The health case is at least as strong as the climate case. Modelling for the West Midlands of England found that city-wide cool roofs could offset roughly 18% of the heat-related mortality attributable to the urban heat island during heatwaves — a figure the EPA highlights because it converts roof colour directly into saved lives.[1][5] And because ground-level ozone forms faster in hot air, cooler cities also tend to have cleaner air on the days that matter most.[1]

The honest limits

Cool surfaces are not free lunch, and a rigorous treatment acknowledges the costs.

The winter penalty is real but usually small: a reflective roof gives up some winter solar gain that would have offset heating. In most climates studied, the penalty is outweighed by summer savings because winter sun is weaker, days are shorter, and snow already whitens many cold-city roofs.[1] Follow-up UK modelling that compared cool roofs’ summer and winter mortality effects across a full year still found substantial net benefit in heat-exposed districts.[6]

Soiling and ageing erode reflectance — a white roof is not white forever without cleaning or recoating. Some reflective coatings carry upfront costs above conventional products, though utility rebate programmes in eleven US states now offset much of that difference.[1]

Glare is the most cited neighbourhood objection: a low-slope white roof visible from upper floors can disturb neighbours, which is one reason cool-coloured (rather than bright white) products matter for acceptance. And cool roofs do nothing for stormwater, biodiversity, air quality or amenity — benefits that vegetation provides and pavement-colour changes cannot, a comparison developed in the third article of this series.

Codes, rebates and the policy machine

Because a roof is replaced only every few decades, policy concentrates on the replacement moment. As of the EPA’s most recent count, cool-roof requirements are mandatory in at least 13 US cities and counties and seven states plus the District of Columbia, largely for new and re-roofed low-slope buildings. Thirty-five utility and municipal rebate programmes across eleven states subsidise reflective roofing, making incentives the most common instrument. Eight voluntary programmes, including LEED, recognise cool roofs as well.[1]

The strategy has spread well beyond the United States. Coating programmes in Indian cities, requirements in European building codes, and the iconic whitewashed roofs of Greek islands — vernacular architecture that anticipated the physics by centuries — all express the same principle: in a hot climate, dark surfaces are a design error.

Beyond roofs: walls and pavements

Roofs are the beachhead. Cool walls perform comparably to cool roofs in California and US climate zones 1–4, especially on older, poorly insulated buildings where wall area dominates envelope heat gain.[1] Reflective and lighter pavements, permeable alternatives, and shade structures over asphalt are the next frontier; the EPA documents each in its compendium of heat-island strategies.[7] Materials science keeps extending the ceiling: laboratory “ultra-white” paints now reflect on the order of 98% of sunlight, and engineering estimates suggest widespread use of such coatings could cool surfaces well beyond what ordinary white paint achieves.[8]

The synthesis for a city is straightforward: every surface is a sunlight decision, made once at construction and revisited at each retrofit. The accumulated record — measured indoor cooling, measured demand reduction, modelled lives saved — supports treating reflectance as a routine, code-enforced property of the urban fabric, like insulation or fire resistance.
