Why Cities Run Hot: The Urban Heat Island, Explained

On a summer afternoon, the middle of a large city is almost always warmer than the fields just beyond its edge. This is not an illusion of traffic and noise: instruments measure it reliably, satellites photograph it from orbit, and electricity grids pay for it every hot afternoon.

Introduction

In the United States, the daytime air in cities runs roughly 1–7°F (about 0.5–4°C) warmer than in surrounding areas, and at night the gap narrows but persists at about 2–5°F.[1] Those averages hide the real story. Between one city block and the next, afternoon temperatures can differ by several degrees, and the hottest neighbourhoods are systematically the ones with the least vegetation and the most pavement. Heat is not distributed evenly by accident, and it is not experienced evenly either: around 489,000 heat-related deaths are recorded worldwide each year, and the toll climbs during the heatwaves that climate change is making longer and hotter.[2]

This article explains what a heat island is, why it forms, how strong it is in practice, and why it matters for health, energy and fairness. It is the first in a series: the articles that follow examine the two main families of remedies — reflective surfaces and urban greenery — and the maps and institutions cities use to manage heat deliberately rather than accidentally.

Two kinds of heat island

Researchers distinguish two related measurements that are often confused.[1]

Surface heat islands describe the temperature of the ground and building materials themselves — asphalt, roofs, car parks — as satellites and infrared cameras see them. Surface differences are the largest by day: dark, unshaded pavement can exceed the air temperature above it by tens of degrees. Conventional roofing on a warm day can run as much as 66°F (about 37°C) hotter than the surrounding air.[1]

Atmospheric heat islands describe the air temperature people actually feel, measured at standard height. The air signal is smaller than the surface signal but steadier: it builds through the day, peaks a few hours after sunset, and persists into the night because buildings and streets release stored heat slowly.[1]

The distinction matters for policy. A city can have dramatic satellite images of hot pavement while its worst public-health problem is the night-time air temperature in dense districts where people cannot cool their bedrooms. The two problems share causes but not always the same fixes.

Why cities run hot: the energy balance

The physical explanation, worked out in foundational work by T. R. Oke and refined since, is that cities alter the surface energy balance in several ways at once.[3]

First, cities replace vegetation with sealed, dark surfaces. A forest or a wet meadow spends a large share of the sun’s energy on evaporation — turning liquid water into vapour, which cools the surface. Asphalt and membrane roofing have no water to evaporate, so nearly all the absorbed sunlight becomes sensible heat: warmth you can feel.

Second, urban materials differ in their radiative properties. Dark surfaces absorb more solar radiation, and deep, massive materials store that energy effectively during the day and release it after sunset.

Third, urban geometry traps both sun and heat. Street canyons — corridors of tall buildings — reflect radiation back and forth between walls, block the cool evening sky from view, and shelter the air from wind. In calm, clear summer weather, when heatwaves are at their worst, this trapping is strongest.[1][3]

Fourth, cities generate their own heat. Vehicles, air-conditioning condensers, industry and lighting all dump waste heat directly into the urban air — and the hotter the city gets, the harder the air-conditioners work, a feedback loop measured on power grids as a 1–9% rise in electricity demand for every 2°F of warming.[1]

How strong is the effect in real cities?

The heat island is not an exotic laboratory effect; it is one of the most consistently observed features of urban climate. A synthesis of one hundred large Asian and Australian cities found that nearly all exhibit clear heat islands, with intensities that vary with city size, building density and background climate, and that tend to peak in the evening.[4] Reviews of European cities tell the same story.

Within a single city, block-scale differences are striking. A bicycle-mounted measurement campaign that sampled air temperature every five metres across a mid-sized American city found that daytime temperature variability within the urban landscape averaged 3.5°C (range 1.1–5.7°C) on summer days — a range comparable to the difference between a mild and a scorching afternoon forecast for the whole region.[5] In other words, two residents of the same city, on the same day, can live in materially different climates depending on the design of their street.

The effect is strongest in larger, denser cities and in humid regions; wind and clouds suppress it.[1] Paradoxically, the heat island is most dangerous precisely on the calm, clear days of a heatwave, when regional temperatures are already extreme and nothing disperses the city’s extra heat.

Heat, health and the night problem

Extreme heat is among the deadliest of weather hazards, and heat islands amplify it. Between 2000 and 2019, about 489,000 people died each year from heat-related causes, with 45% of those deaths in Asia and 36% in Europe.[2] Europe’s 2003 summer heatwave killed an estimated 70,000 people; the Russian heatwave of 2010 an estimated 56,000 in forty-four days; and the summer of 2022 was linked to more than 61,000 excess deaths across Europe despite half a century of warnings and modern air-conditioning.[2]

Heat mortality in adults over 65 rose roughly 85% between 2000–2004 and 2017–2021 as populations aged and heatwaves intensified.[2] The people most at risk are well described: older adults living alone, people with cardiovascular, respiratory, kidney and mental-health conditions, outdoor and manual workers, infants, and urban poor whose housing offers no relief.[2] Within cities, informal settlements and neighbourhoods without shade or cooling are systematically hotter — cities, as the World Health Organization notes, “are not being designed to minimise the accumulation and generation of urban heat”, and their building choices amplify exposure.[2]

Night-time heat is the particular killer. Human bodies need cooler nights to recover from hot days; consecutive hot nights raise mortality sharply. Because urban materials release stored heat after sunset, the atmospheric heat island is typically strongest at night, landing exactly where it does the most physiological harm.[1][2][3]

Beyond death, heat degrades city life in quieter ways: it raises ground-level ozone, which forms faster in warm sunlight and aggravates asthma; it pushes electricity grids toward brownouts on hot afternoons; and it heats urban streams — stormwater running off hot pavement has been measured as much as 18°F warmer than runoff from forested land, stressing aquatic ecosystems.[1]

The heat is unequally distributed

Heat islands are not socially neutral. A national analysis of the 175 largest US cities found that people of colour, on average, live with higher urban heat island intensity than white residents — a pattern that holds in the overwhelming majority of cities studied and is compounded by income.[6]

The roots are often historical. A study of 108 US urban areas showed that neighbourhoods graded “hazardous” in the 1930s federal redlining maps — typically Black and immigrant areas denied home loans — are today hotter than their non-redlined counterparts in the large majority of cases, with differences reaching about 7°C (over 12°F) in the most extreme cities.[7] Eighty-year-old lending maps predict today’s afternoon temperature because they predicted which neighbourhoods received pavement instead of trees and apartments instead of parks.

This is why modern heat policy treats shade, reflective surfaces and cooling access as questions of equity, not merely of engineering. The coolest neighbourhoods in almost every city are the leafiest and wealthiest; the hottest are usually the poorest.[6][7]

What cities can actually change

The physics of the heat island points directly at its levers. Cities can raise the share of sunlight their surfaces reflect (cool roofs, lighter pavements — the subject of the second article in this series). They can restore the evaporative cooling that vegetation provides and the shade that interrupts radiation before it becomes heat (the third article). And they can measure where heat actually concentrates and organise responses around that map — cooling centres, early-warning systems, heat officers — which is the subject of the fourth.

None of these is speculative. The US Environmental Protection Agency’s comprehensive compendium documents measured reductions in surface and air temperature from each strategy, and cities from Phoenix to Athens to Medellín have deployed them at scale.[8] The consistent lesson is that no single intervention suffices: reflective surfaces work best in dense districts, vegetation cools most where irrigation is available and canopy is continuous, and both must reach the hottest neighbourhoods first if the health burden is to move.

How to read the numbers in this series

A few conventions keep the rest of this series honest. Surface temperatures are not air temperatures; a 120°F parking lot does not mean residents experience 120°F air. Averages across a whole city understate the peaks in the worst blocks. And measured cooling from any strategy depends on scale: a single cool roof changes one building’s energy bill, while thousands of them, plus lighter pavement, measurably lower the air temperature of a district.[8][9]

With those cautions, the scale of the opportunity is real. Heat islands are, in effect, a man-made climate penalty that cities impose on themselves — which means it is a penalty cities can choose to stop paying, neighbourhood by neighbourhood, roof by roof, street by street.
