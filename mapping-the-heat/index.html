Mapping the Heat: How Cities See, Predict and Manage Dangerous Temperatures

You cannot cool what you cannot see. For most of the twentieth century a city knew its temperature from a single airport sensor — an average over an airfield that resembled nothing in the city it nominally described.

Seeing heat from orbit

Thermal satellites transformed the study of urban heat by making surface temperature visible everywhere, repeatedly, for free. Instruments such as Landsat’s thermal band map land surface temperature at a resolution of tens of metres, which is fine enough to distinguish a motorway from the park beside it. NASA’s ECOSTRESS instrument, mounted on the International Space Station, images cities at roughly 70-metre resolution at different times of day, capturing both the afternoon build-up of surface heat and — more valuable for health — how slowly different districts cool after dark.[6]

Two disciplines keep satellite heat maps honest. Surface temperature is not air temperature: satellites see roofs and roads, not the air people breathe, and the relationship between the two varies with materials and time of day. And a single image is a moment: comparisons between cities, or years, require consistent processing of what the research literature calls surface urban heat islands.[1][6]

What the images show, consistently, is structure. Thermal images of virtually any large city reveal a rings-and-corriders pattern — hot industrial and parking districts, cool parks and waterways, warm dense cores, cooler leafy suburbs — that matches the material and canopy analysis of the earlier articles in this series.[1][3]

Ground truth: sensors on bicycles and car windows

Satellites see surfaces; people experience air. The most influential recent air-temperature datasets come from simple instruments moved through the city. In Portland, Oregon, researchers laid the methodological groundwork by combining dense mobile measurements with distributed sensor networks to document systematic intra-urban variation and to ground-truth the models used to predict it.[7]

The method has since been scaled into civic campaigns. In community heat-mapping programmes, volunteers mount sensors on their cars and bicycles and drive set routes in the cool of the morning, the heat of the afternoon and the evening, transecting their own city in a single coordinated day. The result is a three-dimensional picture of heat — which districts heat first, hottest and longest — at street level, produced by the residents who live with the result. Local news coverage of these campaigns, from Salt Lake City to Montgomery County, shows the consistent findings: simultaneous differences of roughly 5–15°F between the coolest and hottest parts of the same city, on the same afternoon, in the same weather.[8][9]

The measurement campaigns settle an argument that averages obscure: the intra-city spread rivals the projected regional warming of the coming decades. A city can, in effect, buy back climate decades for its worst blocks with shade and reflectance.

From temperature to vulnerability: the heat equity map

A heat map becomes policy when temperature is combined with the social data that determines who heat kills. Heat vulnerability indices layer measured or modelled temperature onto the demographics of risk: age, chronic illness, social isolation, air-conditioning access, housing quality, occupation.[2][4][5]

The findings of that literature are among the most robust in urban climate. Analysis across the 175 largest US cities found people of colour live with higher heat island intensity than white residents in the overwhelming majority of cities, a pattern that persists within income groups.[5] The historical study of 108 urban areas showed 1930s redlining grades still predict present-day surface temperature: formerly redlined neighbourhoods are hotter in the large majority of cases, by up to about 7°C in the most extreme cities, reflecting decades of pavement-heavy, canopy-poor investment.[4]

Vulnerability mapping does more than document injustice; it directs resources. Tree planting, cool-roof rebates, cooling centres and outreach all cost money, and indices decide whether they flow to the blocks that need them or to the blocks that ask loudest.

Institutions: heat officers and heat action plans

Maps need someone to answer them. The institutional response has developed along three lines.

Early-warning systems and heat action plans are the established core. A heat action plan defines the triggers (temperature forecasts, humidity, duration), the alerts, and the response ladder: opening cooling centres, extending pool and park hours, checking on isolated residents, protecting outdoor workers, and shifting clinic and utility practice. The World Health Organization treats heat–health action plans and early-warning systems as core adaptation infrastructure, alongside guidance for health institutions.[2] The world’s most-cited early plan is Ahmedabad, in India, adopted after a devastating 2010 heatwave; evaluations credit it with measurable reductions in heat mortality, while subsequent analysis emphasises that a plan must keep renewing itself — thresholds age as the climate warms, and initial funding attention fades.[10]

Dedicated heat governance is the newer layer. Beginning in 2021 with the appointment of the world’s first chief heat officers in Miami-Dade County and Athens, cities have begun assigning executive responsibility for heat — someone whose job is to notice that June broke records and to make the bureaucracy respond. Athens’ first chief heat officer, Eleni Myrivili, has described the three-part logic of modern heat governance: name and rank heatwaves so the public treats them as emergencies, cool the city with shade and water and materials, and protect the most exposed residents with targeted outreach.[11] The model has since spread across Europe, North America and Africa.

Neighbourhood-scale intervention is the physical layer where mapping, equity and engineering converge: shade structures over waiting areas and schoolyards, cool roofs and cool corridors in the hottest census tracts, misting and drinking fountains, and rescue programmes for the outdoor workers whose exposure is occupational rather than incidental.

The discipline of follow-through

The hardest part of heat governance is not the map or the plan; it is the follow-through. Evaluations of heat action plans repeatedly find the same failure modes: warning systems that reach smartphones but not the isolated elderly; cooling centres that open too late, close too early, or sit beyond a heat-exhausted walk; plans written for a past climate whose trigger thresholds no longer distinguish dangerous from ordinary summers; and programmes funded by one grant cycle, staffed by none.[2][10]

The cities that do better institutionalise measurement: tracking not just temperature but heat-related emergency calls and mortality by neighbourhood; auditing who actually reaches cooling centres; and re-deriving thresholds every few years. Heat governance succeeds when the map is treated not as a publication but as a dashboard — continuously updated, publicly visible, and attached to budgets.

A series in summary

Taken together, the four articles of this series describe a closed loop. Cities heat themselves through material choices — dark surfaces, sealed ground, waste heat (article one). Two families of physical remedies — reflectance and vegetation — measurably reverse that choice, at building, block and district scale (articles two and three). And the governance layer — satellites, street-level sensors, vulnerability indices, officers and plans — decides where the remedies land and whether they are maintained (this article).

The loop is closing in a growing number of cities, and the direction of travel is unmistakable: heat is moving from the weather report to the zoning code. What remains contested is speed and fairness — whether the coolest streets of 2050 belong to everyone, or once again to the neighbourhoods that always got the trees first.[4][5]
